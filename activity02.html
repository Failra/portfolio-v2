Activity 2: Object Redesign — GhostArm

In this challenge each group was dealt two cards: an everyday object and a format to force it into. My group — Pedro, Parviz, Kerem, and me — drew the robotic arm as our object and invisible / see-through / transparent as our format. The task was to redesign the object so it genuinely fit that format, then work out who would want it, how it would be built, and what problems we hadn't solved.

The redesign. We called it the GhostArm. Instead of the usual painted metal housing, the arm's outer shell is a skin of very thin micro-OLED panels. An array of LiDAR, micro-cameras, and other sensors reads the environment all the way around the arm and relays that information to a computer, which drives the panels so that each face of the arm displays whatever is behind it. From the viewer's side the arm appears to disappear into the room.

The client. Our main client is the entertainment industry — magicians, Disney, theaters, and similar venues that want a prop or effect to move without a visible machine driving it. The same idea also has uses in medical, military, and clean-room settings.

Development and cost. Manufacturing shifts from machining and painting a housing to fabricating a curved micro-OLED outer shell and integrating the sensor array and processing behind it. Cost goes up substantially: high-density OLED panels, high-resolution cameras, the processors to drive them in real time, and the structure to support all of it. We think that's justified for the entertainment client, because being able to use a moving prop without editing the machinery out of the shot afterward saves both time and money — less post-production overall.

Cons and open problems. Pixel density isn't high enough yet to make the effect fully convincing up close. Bendable panels are also a much harder design problem than flat ones, so the less geometrically simple parts of the arm — the joints and curves — would still be the most visible parts of it.
