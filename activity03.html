Activity 3: Paper Airplane — The Peregrine

For this activity I used generative AI to design a paper airplane rather than folding one from memory. I asked for a glider built for time aloft instead of raw speed, and worked from the build it gave me. The result is called the Peregrine.

The design. The Peregrine is a Nakamura-lock glider with swept wings, winglets, and adjustable elevons — deliberately not a dart. It flies slow, flat, and long. The locked nose is the key feature: it holds the plane's shape in the air instead of unfolding mid-flight, which is why competitive throwers use this design for time-aloft events.

What you need. One sheet of standard 8.5×11 paper (20–24 lb copy paper is ideal — printer paper is too flimsy, cardstock too heavy), a hard flat table, and a fingernail or bone folder for sharp creases.

The build.

  1. Center crease. Lay the sheet portrait. Fold it in half the long way (left edge to right edge), crease hard, then open it flat again. This line is the reference for everything that follows.
  2. Corners to the center. Fold the top two corners in so their edges meet exactly along the center crease. You now have a classic pointed nose. Accuracy here decides whether the plane flies straight or banks — check that both flaps are symmetrical before creasing.
  3. Blunt the nose. Fold the sharp point straight down so it lands about 2 cm above the bottom edge of the two triangular flaps. The top of the paper is now a flat horizontal edge. This moves weight forward and kills the dart tendency.
  4. Corners in again. Fold the two new top corners diagonally down to the center crease, same as step 2. Leave roughly 1.5 cm of the folded-down nose triangle poking out below where the two flaps meet — do not cover it.
  5. Lock it. Take that exposed triangle tip and fold it up over both flaps. It clamps them shut. This is the Nakamura lock, and it's the whole reason this plane outperforms a taped dart.
  6. Fold in half. Fold the plane in half along the original center crease, with the lock on the outside. If the lock ends up on the inside, you've folded the wrong way and the nose will pop open on impact.
  7. Wings. Lay it on its side. Fold the top layer down so the wing's leading edge runs roughly parallel to the bottom of the body, leaving about 2.5 cm of body depth below the wing root. Flip and repeat, matching the first fold exactly.
  8. Winglets. Fold the outer 2 cm of each wingtip straight up, perpendicular to the wing. These kill wingtip vortices and stop the plane from rolling out of level flight.
  9. Elevons. At the back edge of each wing, pinch a small tab about 2 cm wide and bend it up maybe 5 degrees. These are the trim controls.
